Exploring what 709 survey respondents think about climate change and threats to oceans and coastlines, how their concerns differ, and how we might turn those concerns into action.

MAIN RESEARCH QUESTION

What do people generally prioritize most when it comes to the World's oceans and coastlines and how climate change affects them?

01 · Who is actually represented here?

Who answered the survey? Identify the most represented countries, demographic groups, and relationships with the ocean.

02 · What do they think is a threat?

How serious do respondents consider the potential threats of climate change? What about threats to marine and coastal ecosystems?

03 · What can we do with this?

ABOUT THE SURVEY

Understanding how people see
the rapidly changing oceans.

This project primarily uses a public-awareness survey developed by Catarina Fonseca and her colleagues working on MaCoBioS (Marine Coastal Ecosystems Biodiversity and Services in a Changing World).

What's the point?

The aim was to understand how people perceive climate change, the value of marine and coastal ecosystems, the pressures affecting them, and options for their sustainable management. They identified a shortage of data connecting these perceptions.

By examining these views together, they aimed to provide evidence that could inform decisions and actions for marine and coastal management.

How did it work?

The voluntary online survey ran from 16 November 2021 to 16 February 2022 in English, French, Spanish, and Italian, yielding 709 responses.

Its 20 questions covered climate-change perceptions; the value of and threats to coasts, oceans, and wildlife; responses to climate change; and respondents’ backgrounds. Recruitment particularly targeted people who already had some interest in climate change and marine and coastal ecosystems.

01 / EXPLORATORY DATA ANALYSIS

Diving headfirst
into the data!

This analysis describes the people who answered the survey. It connects sample composition, independently rated threats, prompted impact selections, and brief written climate concerns.

Who can these results speak for? Recruitment was very targeted towards those working closely with the MaCoBioS project. This is, again, not represenative of the global public, national populations, or even everyone interested in climate change. But it's still good to know who was reached out to.
Q14–Q18 / SAMPLE COMPOSITION

Recreational ocean enjoyers and highly educated respondents are prominent.

Those who primarily interact with the oceans and coasts recreationally account for 376 of 709 respondents (53.0%); conservation, management, and scientific respondents account for 233 (32.9%). Ages 18–40 represent 56.6%, while 18–30 alone represents 30.5%.

The five countries with the most amount of respondents (Italy, France, Ireland, the United Kingdom, and Spain) account for 554/709 (78.1%). The top ten countries account for 625/709 (88.2%).

Q7 / PERCEIVED THREAT SEVERITY

Climate change and plastic pollution both receive high threat ratings.

Using high + very high as the comparison rule, climate change leads overall at 87.2%, followed by plastic pollution at 85.6%. Fishing/harvesting receives 77.2% and chemical pollution 72.5%.

Q9 × Q15 / SELECTED CLIMATE IMPACTS

Extreme weather, sea-level rise, and coastal erosion lead overall.

Respondents most often select changes in extreme-weather frequency (49.8%), sea-level rise (46.5%), and coastal erosion (39.9%). Sea-temperature change follows at 34.8%, and species-distribution change at 32.6%.

Q5 / EXPLORATORY TEXT CODING

Broad climate concerns extend beyond the sea.

Keywords in up to three answers were matched to respondents, which keeps answers separate during matching and counts each person once per category. Of 709 respondents, 704 have retained nonempty text. This includes vague responses, place names, and expressions of no concern; it is not a count of validated, interpretable concerns.

Read these as keyword-category estimates. Categories overlap and differ in breadth. Sixteen retained respondents have no match.

The audit demonstrates specific errors and ambiguity; it cannot estimate precision or recall. An answer’s match here is not necessarily its respondent’s complete set of matches across all three answers. No claim about within-country consistency is made without country-level text summaries.

Proposed coding rules and next validation step
  • Temperature: include explicit warming, unusual warmth, or temperature change; add “warm days in winter.” Exclude unspecified “change.”
  • Ecosystems: include explicit species, habitat, or biodiversity harm. Code forest fires as fires; add ecosystems only when ecological harm is explicit. Marine and terrestrial references should be distinguished.
  • Action/policy: include explicit mitigation, adaptation, or governance responses; move political instability to social consequences unless the answer also proposes a policy.
  • Hazards: retain flood, fire, and other hazard labels separately; flag geological hazards as possible attribution uncertainty, not established climate mechanisms.
  • No concern / insufficient detail: keep explicit “not concerned” separate from vague terms, place names, and missing answers.

These are proposed revisions, not applied recoding. Before updating totals, independently review a documented random sample of matched and unmatched answers, resolve disagreements, and record changes to the codebook. The seven selected examples are diagnostic and not an accuracy estimate.

Within this sample, climate change and plastic pollution both attract strong severity ratings. Extreme weather, sea-level rise, and erosion are frequently selected impacts. Written answers broaden the picture but require more thourough analysis before any strong conclusions can really be drawn.

Conclusion: Among the 709 survey respondents, climate change and plastic pollution were widely rated as serious threats to marine and coastal ecosystems. Extreme weather, sea-level rise, and coastal erosion were the most frequently selected climate impacts. Responses varied across countries and relationships with the ocean, suggesting useful directions for further investigation. Because recruitment was targeted and uneven, these findings describe this sample rather than worldwide public opinion. They provide a pretty solid foundation for testing communication strategies to drive people to contribute more to the wellbeing of the ecosystems they love.

03 / POTENTIAL A/B TEST

Can a message turn
interest into participation?

Does emphasizing coastal protection or marine biodiversity lead to more volunteer registrations for the same coastal-restoration project? A recruiting campaign in Italy (due to having the most respondents) could test this question using two messages motivated by the survey analysis.

The analysis identifies concerns that could inform campaign messages. A randomized experiment would test whether emphasizing one of those concerns changes participation. The survey itself does not establish which message will work better.

These findings concern the overall survey sample and its ocean-relationship groups, not estimates for an Italian campaign audience. Q9’s prompted selections and Q5’s keyword matches have different questions and denominators; their percentages cannot be compared as competing message preferences.

Hypothesis: Coastal-protection framing (A) produces more completed volunteer registrations than marine-biodiversity framing (B), motivated by the prominence of physical-impact concerns in Q9. This is a hypothesis to test, not a conclusion from the survey.

A positive result supports a particular message for the tested audience and setting. It does not, by itself, show that restoration is effective, establish which environmental concern people fundamentally value most, or establish public policy preferences.
